System enablers / Review and monitoring

Burning is measured two ways.
Each tells a different story.

A fire count shows what a satellite saw as it passed. Burnt area shows what changed across a season. Reading Punjab’s progress correctly needs both, and a clear view of what each one misses.

01 / The 2025 result

Punjab’s progress is judged by fire counts, and a fire count measures only part of the burning.

Punjab’s 2025 paddy-season count fell 53% from 2024, the lowest recorded under CAQM’s seasonal monitoring. Burnt area is a second way to measure the same season, and it answers a different question.

02 / Counting fires

A fire count records what a satellite sees at the moment it passes.

Polar-orbiting satellites cross Punjab at set times of day. iFOREST’s methodology says small, short-lived or cloud-obscured fires may be missed, and that several observations can come from one event.

Satellite passes

iFOREST lists Terra at 10:30 am, Aqua at 1:30 pm, and NOAA-20 at 1:30 pm and 1:30 am as nominal times, and notes that actual acquisition times can differ because of orbital drift.

Source: iFOREST methodology, data sources table
Recorded detections

In the CREAMS lists, nearly all of Aqua’s 3,441 detections are time-stamped between 15:00 and 16:59. The two VIIRS satellites’ 2,511 fall between 12:00 and 14:59, apart from 51 at night between 01:00 and 03:59. In all, 52 detections are night-time, and none is stamped after 16:33.

Source: CREAMS daily bulletins; GDi analysis
Reported burning

In a CEEW survey of 1,588 farmers in Haryana, 77% of those reporting village burning said it happens between 4 pm and 10 pm.

Source: CEEW, Action Agenda for Mitigating Stubble Burning in Haryana. The survey covers Haryana, not Punjab.

The detection lists end before the reported burning window does. The Punjab lists stop at 16:33, while the Haryana survey places most village burning between 4 pm and 10 pm. A survey of Punjab farmers would be needed to confirm the same pattern here.

iFOREST also publishes daily fire timing from a geostationary satellite that observes every 15 minutes. It shows a relative pattern of detectable activity, not a count of fires, and favours larger, hotter or longer-lasting fires.

SourcesiFOREST Fire Watch methodology ↗ · CREAMS daily bulletins, ICAR-IARI, processed by GDi’s fire dashboard · CEEW, Action Agenda for Mitigating Stubble Burning in Haryana.

03 / Mapping burnt area

Burnt area is mapped from before-and-after images, not from fire detections.

iFOREST estimates burnt area independently of its fire counts, using Sentinel-2 imagery. The satellite revisits the same ground about every five days, so the method compares how each field looked before and after the burning season.

    What iFOREST publishes

    • The satellite and resolution: Sentinel-2 at 20 m, nominal revisit of five days.
    • The index: Normalized Burn Ratio, and its change between images (dNBR).
    • Time windows of 5 to 10 days, and the use of the least-cloudy valid images.
    • State- and season-specific thresholds, applied to cropland only.
    • Each pixel counted once per season.

    What it does not publish

    • The numeric dNBR or NBR thresholds for each state and season.
    • The exact NBR formula and the cloud-masking method.
    • Ground truthing, validation or an accuracy assessment.
    • Uncertainty ranges or confidence intervals.
    • The start and end dates of the analysis window, and a version or update date.

    SourcesiFOREST Fire Watch methodology, sections 2 and 4 ↗, accessed 16 November 2026. The page carries no date or version. The pixel grid is a GDi illustration with invented values; it shows the order of the steps and uses no real thresholds. The description of NBR as a comparison of near-infrared and short-wave infrared light is from iFOREST’s launch webinar, 5 November 2026.

    What burnt area cannot tell you

    It is not a direct fire detection

    Harvesting or tillage can cause a similar change between two images.

    Source: iFOREST methodology, section 6

    Clouds limit coverage

    iFOREST advises checking the share of cropland with valid imagery before reading a result.

    Source: iFOREST methodology, section 4

    The cropland mask sets the denominator

    Percentages of cropland depend on how cropland is defined, so districts and states need context before they are compared.

    Source: iFOREST methodology, section 6

    Accuracy is not published

    The methodology page lists no validation or uncertainty range. At its launch webinar on 5 November 2026, iFOREST presented Punjab’s kharif 2025 burnt area as a range of about 11,000 to about 21,000 sq km. The district file used here totals 11,085 sq km, near the lower end. iFOREST has not published how low and high are defined.

    Source: iFOREST methodology, section 6; iFOREST launch webinar, 5 November 2026

    Independent checks of satellite burnt-area mapping

    CEEW has tested satellite burnt-area mapping in Punjab against ground data. Its 2025 method relied on Sentinel-2 alone and missed burnt pixels because of the five-day revisit. Its 2026 report added commercial imagery between Sentinel-2 passes.

    These are CEEW’s own methods and not iFOREST’s, so they show what is possible and not how accurate iFOREST’s numbers are.

    Sentinel-2 thresholds, 2025 method
    About 75% accurate, catching 59% of burnt pixels.
    Adding 3 m PlanetScope imagery
    82% accurate, catching 54% of burnt pixels, and finding two to five times more burnt fields. Tested over 1,600 sq km of Sangrur and Patiala, 23 October to 7 November 2024.
    Cost
    About INR 1.2 crore per day for all of Punjab.

    SourceCEEW, Improving Crop Residue Burning Monitoring… (2026 report). Figures are as reported by CEEW.

    05 / Reading the evidence

    Read every number with its measure, its window and its unit.

    Five checks make a fire count or a burnt-area figure safe to quote. Each comes from how the measures are built.

    1. Name the measure.

      A fire count lists events or detections. Burnt area is the square kilometres of pixels that changed. They answer different questions and should not be used as substitutes.

      Source: iFOREST methodology, sections 3 and 4
    2. Match the window and the season.

      CREAMS bulletins run from 15 September to 30 November. iFOREST’s fire count window runs from 1 October to 31 December. Compare figures only across the same window.

      Source: CREAMS bulletins; iFOREST district file
    3. State the denominator.

      A percentage of cropland depends on the cropland mask. The district file reports the share of total agricultural area, not of paddy area.

      Source: iFOREST methodology, section 6; iFOREST district file
    4. Treat no detection as no evidence.

      iFOREST states that the absence of a detection does not mean the absence of burning.

      Source: iFOREST methodology, section 6
    5. Use the dashboards for patterns, not for farm-level action.

      iFOREST says its dashboard helps to understand patterns and differences across space and time, and is not a farm-level compliance or enforcement tool.

      Source: iFOREST methodology, section 6

    What better monitoring needs

    Timing beyond fixed passes

    A geostationary satellite observes every 15 minutes, so it can show when in the day detectable fires occur. iFOREST publishes this as daily fire timing.

    Source: iFOREST methodology, sections 2 and 5

    Imagery between Sentinel-2 passes

    CEEW’s PlanetScope test found two to five times more burnt fields, at about INR 1.2 crore per day for all of Punjab.

    Source: CEEW, 2026 report

    Field records next to maps

    Pair field records with burnt-area mapping and fire detections over the same geography and season.

    Source: Stubble Solutions, CRM machinery
