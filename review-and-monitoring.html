System enablers / Review and monitoring

Burning is measured two ways.
Each tells a different story.

A fire count shows what a satellite saw as it passed. Burnt area shows what changed across a season. Reading Punjab’s progress correctly needs both, and a clear view of what each one misses.

01The 2025 Result

Punjab’s progress is judged by fire counts, and a fire count measures only part of the burning.

Punjab’s 2025 paddy-season count fell 53% from 2024, the lowest recorded under CAQM’s seasonal monitoring. Burnt area is a second way to measure the same season, and it answers a different question.

02Counting Fires

A fire count is built from satellite snapshots, and each satellite sees Punjab only briefly.

How a Fire Gets Counted

  1. 1A satellite detects heat

    Sensors on polar-orbiting satellites pick up thermal anomalies from burning in croplands as they pass overhead.

  2. 2Low-confidence detections are removed

    NOAA-20 keeps nominal- and high-confidence detections. MODIS keeps detections with confidence of at least 30%.

  3. 3Only cropland is kept

    A detection counts only if it falls on cropland in a mask derived from ESA WorldCover 2021.

  4. 4Detections are totalled

    Each detection is placed in a district, converted to Indian Standard Time, and totalled by district, season and day.

SourceiFOREST Fire Watch methodology, active fire count method ↗

The Limits of a Fire Count

Small and Short Fires Can Be Missed

Small, short-lived, low-intensity or cloud-obscured fires may not be detected. A satellite sees only the moment it passes.

Source: iFOREST methodology, limitations

One Fire Can Be Counted More Than Once

Several satellite observations can correspond to the same burning event, so the count is not the number of fires.

Source: iFOREST methodology, limitations

A Low Count Is Not Proof of Less Burning

A low satellite-derived count may reflect limited detection as well as genuinely lower activity. Clouds and the atmosphere can reduce the fire signals visible in some periods.

Source: iFOREST methodology, limitations

Districts Need Context Before They Are Compared

Cropland extent, field size, crop types and burning practices all affect what satellites observe.

Source: iFOREST methodology, limitations

03Mapping Burnt Area

Burnt area is mapped from before-and-after images, not from fire detections.

Burnt area is estimated independently of fire counts, using Sentinel-2 imagery. The satellite revisits the same ground about every five days, so the method compares how each field looked before and after the burning season.

    SourcesiFOREST Fire Watch methodology, sections 2 and 4 ↗

    05Reading the Evidence

    Read every number with its measure, its window and its unit.

    Five checks make a fire count or a burnt-area figure safe to quote. Each comes from how the measures are built.

    1. Name the measure.

      A fire count lists events or detections. Burnt area is the square kilometres of pixels that changed. They answer different questions and should not be used as substitutes.

      Source: iFOREST methodology, sections 3 and 4
    2. Match the window and the season.

      CREAMS bulletins run from 15 September to 30 November. The district fire counts used here run from 1 October to 31 December. Compare figures only across the same window.

      Source: CREAMS bulletins; iFOREST district file
    3. State the denominator.

      A percentage of cropland depends on the cropland mask. The district file reports the share of total agricultural area, not of paddy area.

      Source: iFOREST methodology, section 6; iFOREST district file
    4. Treat no detection as no evidence.

      The absence of a detection does not mean the absence of burning.

      Source: iFOREST methodology, section 6
    5. Use the dashboards for patterns, not for farm-level action.

      A satellite dashboard helps to understand patterns and differences across space and time. It is not a farm-level compliance or enforcement tool.

      Source: iFOREST methodology, section 6

    What Better Monitoring Needs

    Timing Beyond Fixed Passes

    A geostationary satellite observes every 15 minutes, so it can show when in the day detectable fires occur.

    Source: iFOREST methodology, sections 2 and 5

    Imagery Between Sentinel-2 Passes

    CEEW’s PlanetScope test found two to five times more burnt fields, at about INR 1.2 crore per day for all of Punjab.

    Source: CEEW, 2026 report

    Field Records Next to Maps

    Pair field records with burnt-area mapping and fire detections over the same geography and season.

    Source: Stubble Solutions, CRM machinery
